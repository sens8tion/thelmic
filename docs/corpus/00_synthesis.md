# Synthesis: the two-state listener against the corpus

> Written 2026-09-08 across strands 01–05. Vocabulary is the project's:
> **P** = "I have understood this pattern", **Q** = "is a new pattern
> emerging?", **surprise** (never "surprisal"), **legible**, and
> **dominate = legible dominance, not loudness**. Numbers are the corpus's;
> where I extrapolate I say so.

## 1. What the corpus confirms

**The two states are real and the brain predicts along them pre-attentively.** Meter is an internal clock fitted to the surface (Povel & Essens; Large & Jones), strong-beat omissions produce bigger error signals than weak-beat additions with or without attention (Bouwer), tonal expectation is a per-event probability (IDyOM), and every one of these has a fast electrophysiological marker (ERAN 150–250 ms, rhythmic MMN 110–130 ms). Surprise is not a metaphor; it is −log₂ p per event, and listeners' ratings track it.

**Boredom is a clock and the corpus gives it numbers.** Liking peaks around 2 focused exposures and declines by 8–32 (Schellenberg); a 7th verbatim repeat measurably lowers liking (Gold); the unit of attention grows from ~1 s spans to ~6–8 s spans as a piece becomes known (Margulis), so bar-level variation stops being *heard* after a few minutes; EDM changes something at 4/8/16/32-bar boundaries (Butler, Solberg) and practitioners change at every phrase boundary. At 178 BPM a bar is 1.35 s, four bars is 5.4 s — the edge of the perceptual present — and eight bars is 10.8 s, past it. Working rule: **4–8 unchanged repeats of a 4-bar unit is the disengagement zone; a change every bar is the other failure, because the listener's model never converges.**

**Legible dominance is physiology, not taste.** Below 200 Hz one auditory filter is 25–50 Hz wide, so a 50 Hz kick and a 45 Hz sub sit inside the same filter and cannot be separated by EQ; forward masking spans 100–200 ms; the *lowest* stream owns the timing reference (Hove) and bass boosts beat-locked neural responses (Lenc). So kick+sub dominance is temporal and pattern-based: onset survival (≥30 ms clear of the other), anti-correlated envelopes (sidechain is Bregman's old-plus-new heuristic enforced by hand), and a beat-locked share of low-band modulation energy — all loudness-independent. Your rule falls straight out of the numbers.

**Chopping vs holding a break** (the MOEDOR question) is exactly the schematic/episodic distinction: a continuous Amen has moderate schematic syncopation but zero episodic surprise after two loops; grid chops on 1- and 3-unit cuts that keep the downbeat re-inject surprise per phrase while precision in the meter stays high. 16th-note chops at 178 sit below the ~100 ms floor and read as texture, not rhythm — a different, timbral kind of surprise.

## 2. Where the corpus amends the model

**(a) Reward lives in the transition, not in either state.** Dopamine in the caudate during anticipation and in the accumbens at the peak (Salimpoor); the Cheung saddle (a surprising event is pleasant only in a low-uncertainty context; an unsurprising one only in a high-uncertainty context); skin conductance peaking at the drop, not the build (Solberg & Dibben); audience EEG synchrony rising *before* the climax and falling *at* it (Kaneshiro). P and Q are both places you pass through. This means **dwell in P is not waste — it is the investment that makes the next surprise legible.** A pattern held for 8 bars earns a bigger disturbance than one held for 2. Your "repeat 3 bars, disturb the 4th" is the small-scale version of this; the corpus says the same works at 8/16/32.

**(b) Surprise must be precision-weighted.** Vuust's PCRI: a syncopation against a firm meter is a big signal; the same syncopation against a collapsed meter is discounted, because the listener no longer trusts the model it violates. Witek's inverted-U and Fitch & Rosenfeld's phase resets are the behavioural face of this. So "illegible" is not "very surprising" — **illegible material generates *less* effective surprise than legible material with one thing wrong.** This is why a grating filter ramp or a 1/96 beat-repeat reads as noise rather than as a big event: it removes the pattern that surprise is measured against.

**(c) Trance is a third mode, not a long dwell in P.** During trance the auditory pathway *decouples* from the drum (Hove), detection thresholds rise after minutes of regular music (Lanzilotti), and trait absorption predicts the phenomenology while neural entrainment does not (Aparicio-Terrés). The Q-monitor doesn't keep answering "no"; it goes quiet, and the body is held by other channels: vestibular bass above ~90 dB(A), sub-threshold VLF (+12% movement), synchrony with other dancers. Conditions: a near-isochronous pulse (6.7 ms jitter was trance, 16.5 ms was the control), roughly ten continuous minutes or more, and bodily hold. **A 4-minute track cannot induce it; it can only sustain or break one a set has established.** For a single track, the boredom clock is the law.

**(d) Return is its own event.** After absence, the reprise of a known pattern is rewarded (Cheung's high-uncertainty/low-surprise quadrant; Huron's prediction reward; Margulis's 6-s attention units). It should not be modelled as "P again"; it is a Q→P transition whose payoff scales with how uncertain the preceding section made things, and decays with the count of prior returns. Recognisability is carried mostly by the **vocal** (Hooked: vocal prominence is the largest predictor; low melodic entropy helps) — which is why your vocal rules (max 8 bars, blue ones sparingly, never end on a part-played one) are doing structural work, not just taste.

## 3. The build/drop, restated

The build manufactures uncertainty about **when**, with near-certainty about **what**: kick and bass removed (the timing anchor gone), risers and snare subdivision that never settle into a pattern of their own. The drop is a low-surprise event in a high-uncertainty context — Cheung's pleasant quadrant — and its payload must be legible within one bar (kick + bass back on the grid, at or above pre-break level). Measured builds are 8–16 bars; practitioners cap at 32; the decisive uncertainty is concentrated in the *last bar* (Turrell's EEG shows nothing distinctive 4 s earlier). A build that stops delivering a new small regularity every ~8 bars curdles: risers alone are stationary noise (no learning progress — Schmidhuber's definition of boring), and an over-long roll at constant subdivision becomes a pattern of its own and cashes the tension without a payoff. The breakdown's job is only to *cut* the pulse-carrying layers and reset the clock; contrast in material helps but is optional.

Groove, inside the core, is micro-cycling within P: a few off-grid events per bar (index ~4 of Witek's scale; 18 collapses it) against an unambiguous kick; microtiming is not the lever (quantised patterns groove as well as played ones — Senn; Danielsen: shape the *sound*, not the offsets). Vary timbre and envelope, not onsets.

## 4. What can be measured, and on whom

From a bounce or the MIDI, without a listener: per-lane rhythmic surprise (LHL schematic × an episodic onset table with a 2–4-cycle half-life × meter confidence), tonal surprise (Krumhansl–Kessler key clarity + an IDyOM-style short-term model, reported as within-piece percentiles), legible-dominance metrics for kick+sub (onset survival, low-band envelope correlation, beat-locked modulation ratio), and a dwell clock per lane. From these: a **boredom map** (low surprise, low uncertainty, dwell past threshold) and a **chaos map** (high surprise, high uncertainty, sustained) over the arrangement.

On a person: skin conductance 1–3 s after a structural event is the clearest single-listener marker of a Q→P transition; beat-rate phase-locking (fronto-central EEG) indexes legibility; HRV separates boredom from engagement at the minute scale. All of it within-person only, seated, over ≥5 sessions. Do not use frontal-midline theta, alpha asymmetry, binaural anything, or a forehead headband's "flow score". Group synchrony (EEG ISC) needs a room of people hearing the same track.

## 5. Recommendation

Build the ear first, calibrate it on what you like, and only then put sensors on you. Three options, in the order I'd do them:

**A. `thelmic/ouvido` — the surprise/dwell trace.** Extend `hear.py` from per-bar energy to per-bar *surprise* and *dwell* per lane, plus the kick+sub legibility metrics, producing the boredom and chaos maps. Test it immediately against assertions you have already made about segura: drop 2 reads bigger than its level (arrival contrast); CINZA was boring; the early subs had too much tonal movement; the 1/96 beat repeat and the FACA ramp were noise, not surprise; the second drop's kick was thin. If the maps don't reproduce those calls, the model is wrong and we find out cheaply.

**B. Calibrate on the tracks you like.** Run A over a folder of your reference tracks and read off where *their* dwell times, surprise budgets and drop shapes sit. This is the "assert qualitative things to measurables" step: thresholds fitted to your ear, not to Billboard.

**C. A one-person rig (later).** Polar H10 + a finger EDA sensor, seated, own tracks plus a measure-shuffled control and a deliberately over-held loop, five sessions. Only worth doing once A and B exist to compare against.

Cost of A is a day or two of code on top of `hear.py`; B is a batch run; C is hardware and weeks. The "bigger problems" in segura are, I'd guess, going to be things A can point at directly — dwell too long on a lane, a surprise that isn't legible, a drop whose payload isn't on the grid within a bar.
