Framing note. Everything below is related back to the two-state listener model: a listener travels between "I have understood this pattern" and "is a new pattern emerging?", and too long in either state produces boredom. The literature's most useful contribution turns out to be that trance is not simply a long dwell in the "understood" state; it is a distinct mode in which the *monitoring for new pattern is downregulated* while bodily engagement is held by non-cognitive channels. Section (d) develops this.

## (a) Core claims, with evidence quality

**1. Rhythmic "auditory driving" of brain rhythms is the cause of trance (Neher 1961/62). Debunked as a causal account.**
Neher exposed 10 subjects to drum sounds at 3, 4, 6 and 8 beats/s for ~4 minutes each, observed EEG phase-coupling in auditory cortex and eye-blink synchrony in half the subjects, and extrapolated to ceremonial trance, predicting drum rhythms "slightly below 8 to 13 cycles per second" in ceremonies. Critiques: tiny sample, 4-minute stimulus, no statistics, no time-of-day control (summarised in the Gordon et al. 2026 and Aparicio-Terrés et al. 2025 literature reviews). Rouget (1985, pp. 172–6) made the field-side objection that ceremonial drumming rates are nowhere near 8–13 Hz and that if Neher were right "half of Africa would be in a trance" all year. Will & Berg (2007) re-tested with 1–8 Hz stimulation and found cortical synchronisation only in the delta range, peaking around 2 Hz — i.e., the brain tracks beats, but that tracking is not a trance switch.

**2. Beat-tracking in the brain correlates weakly with altered-state proxies. Weak-to-moderate (new, small-N).**
Gordon, Karvat, Dagan & Landau (2026, N=40): 10-minute drumming at delta/theta/alpha rates; theta (4 bps) neural tracking correlated with self-reported altered experience, and strong theta trackers reported higher ASC across all rhythms — read as a *susceptibility marker*, not a driver. Aparicio-Terrés et al. (2025, N=19): one-minute EDM excerpts at 99/135/171 BPM; entrainment and "experience of unity" were both highest at 99 BPM; brain–behaviour correlations R² < 0.10, authors advise caution. Hove et al. (2016, fMRI, 15 shamanic practitioners, 8-min scans, 4.24 Hz drumming): trance was associated with *reduced* connectivity in the auditory pathway ("perceptual decoupling") and stronger default-mode/control hubs. Critically, the non-trance control used the same drumming with more timing jitter (SD of inter-onset interval 16.5 ms vs 6.7 ms) — predictability was the manipulated variable.

**3. Binaural beats / "brainwave entrainment" products. Debunked for entrainment and mood; weak for cognition.**
López-Caballero & Escera (2017): beats at 4.5–57 Hz produced no EEG band enhancement and no arousal change. Orozco Perez et al. (2020): binaural beats entrain cortex weakly, monaural beats more; neither affected mood. Ingendoh et al. (2023 systematic review): 5 studies support, 8 contradict, 1 mixed; heterogeneity prevents conclusions. A 2019 meta-analysis (Garcia-Argibay et al.) found a medium effect on cognition/anxiety but across highly heterogeneous designs.

**4. Heart rate entrains to tempo ("trance BPM ≈ heart rate"). Debunked / unsupported.**
Mütze, Kopiez & Wolf (2020) found "no evidence for entrainment or synchronization" of heart rate to a djembe pulse, even with a closed feedback loop. Juslin et al. (2026) title the hypothesis "plausible… in need of evidence". No source was found for any specific trance BPM tied to cardiac rhythm; such claims appear only on blogs.

**5. Music is neither necessary nor sufficient for trance; it triggers and maintains a culturally learned state. Strong (anthropological consensus).**
Rouget (1985): music's relation to trance is "neither causal nor deterministic"; its main function is to *maintain* trance and mark its phases; each culture couples music and trance differently. Becker (2004): trancing is emotional and learned through a "habitus of listening"; the same physiological capacity is expressed as deep listening in secular contexts. Herbert (2011): diary study, 20 participants over two weeks, plus Tellegen absorption scale; absorption/dissociation ("trancing") is ubiquitous in everyday listening and self-regulatory, with large individual differences. Vaitl et al. (2005) classify rhythm-induced trance as a physiologically evoked ASC but note the mechanism is assumed rather than shown.

**6. Prolonged, monotonous, predictable rhythm produces absorption and reduced sensory gating. Moderate.**
Aparicio-Terrés et al. (2025 review): the evidence "aligns with the notion that being exposed to these sounds facilitates a state of absorption and relaxation". Lanzilotti et al. (2019): many minutes of *continuous* trance music raised auditory detection thresholds and shrank ERPs to targets, more so for regular than scrambled music, with increased frontal–central alpha connectivity — the opposite of the perceptual sharpening seen for short rhythmic streams. Kjellgren & Eriksson (2010, 20-min sessions, N=22) and Woodside et al. (1997) report vivid imagery and ASC phenomenology from monotonous drumming in non-experts. Kontouli, Hove, Lehmann, Vuust & Keller (2026 review): trance across shamanism and rave shares "a common set of musical features" and partially overlapping neural dynamics (low-frequency synchronisation; shift from executive to limbic/default networks), but evidence "remains fragmented and methodologically heterogeneous".

**7. Dancing to electronic music induces measurable ecstatic trance without drugs. Moderate (single field study).**
Schäfer & Kreuzburg (2022): 91 clubgoers surveyed after several hours of techno dancing, Phenomenology of Consciousness Inventory; trance depth equivalent in drug and non-drug groups. The paper lists facilitating features: high loudness, increasing tempo, crescendos/accelerandos, repetition, little variation, prolonged duration.

**8. Loud low-frequency content engages the body outside audition. Moderate.**
Todd & Cody (2000, N=10): techno above ~90 dB(A) evokes vestibular (sternocleidomastoid) responses — a physiological basis for the "rock and roll threshold". Cameron et al. (2022): at a live 45-minute electronic concert, toggling very-low-frequency speakers every two minutes raised audience movement by ~12% although the VLF was undetectable — an unconscious vestibular/tactile channel.

**9. Interpersonal synchrony raises pain threshold and social closeness. Strong.**
Tarr, Launay & Dunbar (2015; 2016 "silent disco"): synchronous dancing, independent of exertion, raises pain thresholds (endorphin proxy) and self-other bonding. Tarr et al. (2014) review the mechanisms. This is not trance per se but supplies the "self-other merging" component reported in rave ethnographies (Hutson 2000; Takahashi & Olaveson 2003; St John).

**10. The breakdown/build-up/drop routine reliably produces peak responses. Moderate.**
Solberg (2014): techniques are uplifters (rising white-noise/pitch), the "drum roll effect" (snare subdivision accelerating), large frequency changes, removal and reintroduction of kick and bass, and a contrasting breakdown. Solberg & Jensenius (2017, N=16, motion capture): group movement changes sharply at breakdown, build-up and drop; drop rated most pleasurable. Solberg & Dibben (2019, N=24, lab): drop sections show significantly higher skin conductance than other sections, even seated. Grosz et al. (2025) formalise Intro/Breakdown–Build-up–Core–Outro as a tension–release narrative grammar.

**11. Repetition reshapes attention and pleasure. Strong (lab), moderate (transfer to dancefloor).**
Margulis (2014): with repeated exposure, listeners lose sensitivity to 1-second repeats and gain sensitivity to ~8-second repeats — "an attentional shift from more local to more global levels"; inserting literal repetition into atonal music raised liking. Hunter & Schellenberg (2011): liking is an inverted-U over 2/8/32 exposures at group level, moderated by Openness. Witek et al. (2014): medium syncopation maximises wanting-to-move and pleasure (inverted-U). Cheung et al. (2019): pleasure peaks for low-uncertainty/high-surprise events *and* high-uncertainty/low-surprise events. Garcia (2005): EDM pleasure is "process pleasure" from an "ever-changing same"; the listener's standing question is "when will it change next, and how will it still remain the same?", with changes expected on multiples of 4 (beats, bars, 8 bars). Garcia explicitly does not treat trance or boredom.

**12. Flicker/strobe produces visual hallucinatory phenomena. Moderate (lab), untested in clubs.**
Allefeld et al. (2011): stroboscopic light (1–50 Hz scan) produces geometric percepts most strongly around alpha (8–14 Hz). Vaitl et al. (2005) treat sensory homogenisation/deprivation as an ASC induction class; darkness on the dancefloor is documented ethnographically (Rietveld's "disappearance from the self") but not experimentally.

**13. A "trance tempo" exists. Weak / over-generalised.**
Moelants (2002): spontaneous motor tempo clusters at 120–130 BPM (500 ms period), supporting the van Noorden & Moelants resonance model; this is a *movement* preference, not an ASC finding. Trance-inducing musics span 99 BPM (best entrainment in Aparicio-Terrés) to 240 BPM (shamanic 4 Hz), so tempo per se is not the variable; predictability, continuity and duration are.

## (d) Implications for a listener-model based on surprise and dwell time

**Trance is a third mode, not a long dwell in "understood".** The two-state model predicts that a listener who has understood the pattern and finds no new pattern emerging will get bored and leave. The trance literature describes a state in which the same input produces the opposite outcome: the listener stays and stops asking. Hove et al. show the auditory pathway *decoupling* from the drum during trance; Lanzilotti et al. show detection thresholds rising and evoked responses shrinking after many minutes of continuous regular music. In the model's terms, the "is a new pattern emerging?" monitor is downregulated rather than repeatedly answered "no". Boredom is the monitor firing and finding nothing; trance is the monitor going quiet while the body stays engaged. The design question therefore is not "how long can the listener hold a pattern before disengaging" but "what keeps the listener attached while the surprise stream approaches zero".

**Conditions the evidence supports for entering the mode.**
1. *Full legibility of the pulse, held steady.* Hove's manipulation is the sharpest datum: the same 240 BPM drum with jitter raised from 6.7 to 16.5 ms SD was enough to be used as the non-trance control. Legibility of the pulse (distinct, unambiguous, independent of loudness) is a precondition; micro-timing looseness works against trance even when it improves groove. This is separate from Witek's medium-syncopation optimum, which concerns wanting-to-move rather than absorption; a trance-oriented lane should keep the timekeeping layer near-isochronous and put syncopation in other layers.
2. *Continuity and duration.* Lab inductions use 8–20 continuous minutes; field trance reports follow hours. Intermittent presentation abolishes the threshold effect. A track under 5 minutes cannot itself carry a listener into the mode; it can only sustain or interrupt a mode a set has already established. Model implication: dwell time for a trance-lane pattern should be measured in minutes-to-tens-of-minutes, not bars, and the model should track *cumulative* continuous exposure across tracks.
3. *A non-cognitive channel that holds engagement while surprise is low.* Bass above ~90 dB(A) engages the vestibular system; undetectable VLF raises movement 12%; synchronous movement with others raises pain threshold and self-other merging. These reward the body without asking the pattern monitor anything. This is why the literature repeatedly lists loudness and bass among facilitating features (Schäfer & Kreuzburg) though loudness is not part of legibility. The model needs a "bodily hold" term alongside surprise: when bodily hold is high, tolerable dwell time at near-zero surprise extends greatly; when it is low (home listening at moderate volume), the ordinary boredom curve applies.
4. *Individual susceptibility and learned habitus.* Gordon et al. (theta-tracking as a trait marker), Herbert (absorption trait), Becker and Rouget (culturally learned expectation of trance). Any single dwell-time constant will be wrong for a large share of listeners; a model should carry a susceptibility parameter.

**How repetition and variation should be paced.** Garcia's "ever-changing same" and Margulis's local-to-global shift together give a pacing rule. Early in exposure the listener tracks 1–2 second units (one bar); after repeated cycles the unit of attention lengthens to 8-second and longer spans (4–8 bars), and small-unit changes stop being noticed. Solberg documents change points at 2/4/8/16/32 bars. So variation should be nested at multiples of 4 and should *migrate outward over time*: bar-level micro-variation in the first minutes, then phrase-level (8–16 bar) changes, then section-level. Variation that stays at bar level after the attention unit has grown is neither surprising nor legible — it is invisible, which is Lanzilotti's threshold effect in compositional form. Variation that jumps inward (a sudden bar-level novelty after long global attention) re-arms the monitor and exits trance. Hunter & Schellenberg's inverted-U (2 → 8 → 32 exposures) suggests that literal, unchanged repetition of a *whole* unit peaks in liking somewhere around 8 exposures for average listeners; keeping the loop identical past that risks the boredom exit unless bodily hold is high.

**What the build/drop is, in the two-state model.** The breakdown removes the pattern (absence: monitor re-arms, the listener travels back to "is a new pattern emerging?"). The build-up manufactures high uncertainty about *when* with near-certainty about *what*: uplifters, snare-roll subdivision, kick and bass removed. The drop restores the pattern at maximum legibility — Cheung's "high-uncertainty, low-surprise" pleasure condition, measurable as the skin-conductance peak in Solberg & Dibben and the movement peak in Solberg & Jensenius. In model terms the drop is a *controlled exit and re-entry*: it spends the accumulated dwell, pays it out as a surprise event whose content is fully understood, and then re-enters the low-surprise mode with the body re-synchronised. Build-ups in analysed tracks run 15–30 seconds; longer builds trade higher uncertainty for the risk that the listener's monitor, having re-armed, gets bored again. Between drops, a set-level model should expect the monitor to be quiet for long stretches and the bodily channel to carry engagement.

**What does not belong in the model.** No "trance BPM": inductions run from 99 BPM (best entrainment in Aparicio-Terrés) through 120–130 (house) and 150–180 (jungle, with bass at half tempo giving a legible slow layer under a fast one) to 240 (shamanic). No heart-rate coupling. No binaural or "brainwave" tricks. Tempo's real role is via the motor system (Moelants 120–130 spontaneous tempo) and via which metric level the body locks to — jungle's half-tempo bass is the same principle as house's four-on-the-floor, read at a different level. What tempo does change is *how many cycles per minute the pattern is confirmed*, which affects how fast the attention unit grows; a faster confirmation rate should shorten the time to the global-attention stage, all else equal, though no study has measured this directly.

**Summary rule for the model.** Trance = legible, near-isochronous pulse + continuous exposure on the order of ten minutes or more + bodily hold (bass, dancing, synchrony) + variation nested at multiples of 4 that migrates from bar to phrase to section as the listener's attention unit grows + periodic engineered resets (breakdown/drop) that discharge the dwell without re-arming bar-level monitoring. Boredom occurs when the monitor re-arms with nothing to find; ordinary engaged listening occurs when it keeps finding something; trance occurs when it is allowed to fall silent while the body is still held.
