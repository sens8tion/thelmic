Framing: the lead's model is that listening oscillates between "I have understood this pattern" and "is a new pattern emerging?", with boredom when either state lasts too long. Below, *surprise* = mismatch between an onset (or an absence) and the listener's metrical prediction; *dwell time* = how long the listener stays in the "understood" state; *legible* = the pattern can be discerned distinctly, independent of loudness.

## (a) Core claims, with evidence quality

**A1. Meter is an internal clock/oscillator fitted to the surface, not a property of the sound.** Povel & Essens (1985) showed listeners induce an internal clock from accent distribution (accents on isolated tones, the second of a pair, the first and last of a run of 3+), and that reproduction accuracy is predicted by how little counter-evidence the best clock meets. Longuet-Higgins & Lee (1984) add the complementary rule: any note sequence is metrically ambiguous, and the listener picks the interpretation with the *fewest syncopations*. Large & Jones (1999) recast this as entrainment of attentional oscillators whose peaks target expected moments; Jones et al. (2002) showed pitch judgements are most accurate for tones arriving at rhythmically expected times. Large, Herrera & Velasco (2015) and Tal et al. (2017) show a pulse is felt even for "missing-pulse" rhythms with no energy at the pulse frequency. **Strong** (converging theory, behaviour, MEG/EEG).

**A2. Metrical positions form a hierarchy and the brain predicts along it, pre-attentively.** Palmer & Krumhansl (1990): goodness-of-fit ratings reveal a multi-level hierarchy of accent strength. Brochard et al. (2003, cited in Vuust 2018): identical metronome clicks are heard as alternating strong/weak. Bouwer et al. (2014): omitting a sound on a strong beat elicits a larger MMN than on a weak beat, with no effect of attention or musical training. Damsma & van Rijn (2017): pupil dilation indexes the metrical hierarchy of *unattended* violations. **Strong.** Implication: surprise is not uniform — an absence on "the one" is a bigger prediction error than an extra hit on an off-16th.

**A3. Meter has hard temporal limits.** London (2004; 2006 lectures; 2023): elements faster than ~100 ms IOI cannot be heard as separate rhythmic events (Hirsh et al. 1990; Bolton: grouping difficult at 158 ms, impossible at 115 ms; Repp 2002 synchronisation threshold ~115 ms); above ~1.5–2.0 s IOI the sense of continuity/beat "falls apart"; spontaneous/comfortable tempo is 500–700 ms (86–120 BPM). **Strong** for these three figures (verified in London's own text). London's book also gives an upper bound for the whole metric cycle (measure) of roughly 5–6 s, tied to the "perceptual present"; not verified from a fetched source — **moderate / from memory of the book**.

**A4. Tempo preference is a resonance near 120 BPM and listeners shift metric level to stay near it.** Van Noorden & Moelants (1999) model tempo salience as a damped oscillator with resonance at 500 ms (120 BPM); Moelants (2002) refines preferred tempo to 120–130 BPM; McKinney & Moelants (2006) show tapped-tempo distributions cluster at whatever metric level lies nearest 120 BPM, with 2:1 ambiguity between levels. London (2023) gives the direct demonstration: "Ode to Joy" at 78 quarter/min is tapped at 78; at 138 quarter/min most listeners tap every other note, feeling a beat at 69 BPM. **Strong.** This is the mechanism behind half-time at 170–180 BPM (see §b).

**A5. Syncopation = prediction error against the metrical model; moderate syncopation maximises groove; too much destroys the meter.** Vuust et al. (2009): isolated syncopations in a drum groove elicit MMNm (~110–130 ms) then P3am, larger in musicians. Vuust & Witek (2014) and Vuust et al. (2018, PCRI model): the effective signal is *precision-weighted* prediction error — low syncopation gives little error, very high syncopation lowers confidence in the meter so the error is discounted; the maximum is in the middle. Witek et al. (2014): inverted U between a polyphonic LHL index and wanting-to-move/pleasure across 50 funk breaks; Witek's follow-up motion-capture: for high syncopation participants "synchronized very poorly". Fitch & Rosenfeld (2007): pulse-tracking error and *phase resets* (listeners re-hearing the off-beat as the beat) rise with syncopation index, independent of tempo. Replicated by Matthews et al. (2019, 2022). **Strong for the mechanism; the inverted U itself is contested**: Senn et al. (2024) found a null quadratic effect (p = .83) with 40 idiomatic drummer patterns and argue earlier U-shapes came partly from unidiomatic experimenter-built patterns ("patterns matter", Witek et al. 2022). Fram & Berger (2023) show the mapping from LHL-type scores to *perceived* syncopation depends on which metrical prime the listener has adopted.

**A6. Syncopation measures built on a weighted metrical hierarchy predict human complexity judgements best.** Thul & Toussaint (2008), comparing ~20 measures against four human datasets (Povel & Essens 1985, Shmulevich & Povel 2000, Essens 1995, Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007): hierarchy-weighted measures (LHL, Keith, metrical complexity, Pressing) cluster with human perceived-complexity ratings; IOI-histogram/entropy measures are poor predictors. **Strong.**

**A7. Meter inference is a probabilistic learning process and improves prediction.** Van der Weij, Pearce & Honing (2017): an IDyOM model that infers meter and phase from onsets lowers per-event information content from 1.54 to 1.34 bits vs a non-metrical model and classifies time signatures at 67% with a first-order context (38% at order 0). **Moderate** (computational, folk-melody corpus).

**A8. Repetition drives modelling; groove music holds syncopation level constant so deviations become expected.** Vuust et al. (2018): rhythm sections "play repeated syncopated rhythmic patterns (grooves), keeping the amount of syncopation relatively constant" and higher levels of the hierarchy "model expected deviations from the meter"; the PCRI paper explicitly leaves veridical (piece-specific) expectation unmodelled. Margulis (2014): repeated exposure shifts attention from short to longer-span repetitions. **Moderate.** No study found gives a clean "N repetitions to learn a rhythm" number (see §b).

**A9. Breakbeats de-emphasise strong beats, and jungle production is anchored by downbeat preservation.** Butler (2006, via Christodoulou 2020): breakbeat rhythms "de-emphasize strong beats, instead placing considerable stress on metrically weak locations". Christodoulou (2020): the listener's search for a basic pulse "generates brief feelings of tension that are temporarily relieved as she learns to anticipate the off-beats through the break-beat's repetition at even points in time"; jungle tempos were ~160 BPM by 1993 with basslines "running at around 80 BPM". Hockman, Davies & Fujinaga (2012): hardcore left the 120–130 BPM house standard for "upwards of 180 BPM"; breakbeats "almost invariably begin with kick drums" and producers "mostly apply downbeat-preserving transformations", re-ordering slices "to preserve the perception of downbeats". Collins (2001): standard cuts are on an 8th-note grid; short odd-length cuts (3+3+2 logic) that resolve at the downbeat. **Moderate** (musicological/ethnographic + MIR, not perceptual experiments).

**A10. Tamborzão.** Only sound-morphology / cultural analyses found (Palombini-style "From Volt Mix to Tamborzão"); producer sources put it at ~130–140 BPM on a triplet-feel grid derived from atabaque/capoeira patterns. **Weak** — no peer-reviewed rhythmic-perception analysis located.

## (b) Measurable parameters, formulas, numbers

**Temporal limits (London 2004/2006/2023).** Rhythmic floor ≈ 100 ms IOI; beat ceiling ≈ 1.5–2.0 s; spontaneous tempo 500–700 ms (86–120 BPM); comfortable tapping 100–120 BPM; resonance peak 500 ms (van Noorden & Moelants, damping 1.12); Moelants 2002 preferred 120–130 BPM. Applied to jungle: at 170 BPM quarter = 353 ms, 8th = 176 ms, 16th = 88 ms, bar = 1.41 s, 4 bars = 5.65 s, 8 bars = 11.3 s; at 180 BPM 16th = 83 ms. Consequences: (i) 16th-note chops sit *below* the 100 ms floor and fuse into texture/rolls rather than being heard as discrete rhythmic events (Collins notes 16th cutups are "more easily interpreted if played against a solid backing beat"); (ii) the 353 ms quarter is felt but is faster than the resonance peak, so the tactus migrates to the 706 ms half-time level (85 BPM) — exactly London's 138→69 case — which is why the two-step's snares on 2 and 4 read as a backbeat at ~85 BPM while hats/breaks supply 8th-note activity at 170; (iii) a 4-bar cycle is at the edge of the ~5–6 s "present", an 8-bar cycle is beyond it and must be held in memory/veridical expectation.

**Longuet-Higgins & Lee syncopation (as implemented by Fitch & Rosenfeld 2007, Witek 2014).** Stratify a 4/4 bar to 16ths and weight positions by the highest level they initiate: downbeat 0; beat 3 = −1; beats 2, 4 = −2; off-8ths = −3; off-16ths = −4. For 16 positions: `[0,−4,−3,−4,−2,−4,−3,−4,−1,−4,−3,−4,−2,−4,−3,−4]`. A syncopation occurs when a rest or tied note at weight w_R is preceded by a sounded note of lower weight w_N; its value is w_R − w_N; sum over the loop (wrap around). Example from Fitch: rest on the downbeat after an off-8th = 0−(−3) = 3. Ranges: 0–15 in Fitch & Rosenfeld's monophonic stimuli; 0–81 in Witek's polyphonic index (adds instrument weights for kick/snare/hat, computed per two-bar pattern). Sioros & Guedes' NLHL-p: sum only positive scores, divide by (pulses per bar − 1); polyphony: take the max score at each position across instruments, then sum.

**Sioros & Guedes amplitude-based measure (ISMIR 2012).** For each pulse: (1) find the metrical levels it belongs to; (2) at each level average the amplitude differences to its two neighbours at that level; (3) take the minimum average as the pulse's raw score; (4) syncopation potential = 1 − 0.5^m, m = highest level the pulse belongs to (bar = 0 so downbeats have potential 0); (5) score × potential; sum and normalise by the maximum for that number of events. Worked example: a 1.0-amplitude hit at pulse 5 with 0.5 neighbours gives 0.75 × 0.5 = 0.375. Useful because it handles velocity-driven syncopation (ghost notes, rolls) that binary LHL ignores.

**Weighted Note-to-Beat Distance (Gómez, Melvin, Rappaport & Toussaint 2005).** Beats e_i are the quarter-note pulses (unit distance 1). For note x, T(x) = min(d(x,e_i), d(x,e_{i+1})) as a fraction of the beat. D(x) = 0 if x is on a beat; 1/T(x) if x ends on or before the next beat; 2/T(x) if it crosses the next beat but ends by the one after; 1/T(x) if it ends later still. WNBD = mean of D(x) over notes. Toussaint's *off-beatness* = number of onsets at positions coprime to the cycle length. Keith's measure: hesitation 1, anticipation 2, syncopation 3. Thul & Toussaint: prefer hierarchy-weighted measures (LHL family) for perceived complexity.

**Groove sweet spots.** Matthews et al. (2019/2022): 4-bar, 5-onsets-per-bar piano patterns with LHL index 0 / 4 / 18 gave groove ratings 41 / 61 / 20 (η² = .46). Witek 2014: two-bar funk breaks at 120 BPM, looped 4× (16 s), peak of the U at medium index. Fitch & Rosenfeld: tempo 75–105 BPM had no effect on syncopation difficulty; resets occur when taps drift to the off-beat, visible after ~10 s.

**Probabilistic surprise (van der Weij et al. 2017).** h(e) = −log₂ p(e | context), with p marginalised over hypothesised meters and phases; metrical position = (onset − φ) mod T_m.

**Repetitions to "learn".** No experiment gives a single number. Constraints: beat induction is fast and pre-attentive (Bouwer 2014); van der Weij's model gains most of its accuracy from a one-event context; Fitch & Rosenfeld gave 46 pulses of exposure (~12 bars) and still saw resets for high-syncopation patterns; Witek's stimuli were heard 4 loops; London's "shave and a haircut" demonstration shows a whole pattern is predicted on first hearing when familiar. Working figure (inference, not measurement): a 1-bar pattern is entrained within 1–2 cycles and fully modelled by ~4; a 2-bar pattern by ~4 cycles (8 bars).

**Phrase lengths.** EDM (Butler 2006; Smith 2021 MTO; Solberg & Dibben 2019): duple hypermeter at 4/8/16/32 bars; uplifters/downlifters mark hypermetric downbeats and are larger at more important ones; break routines ~20 s. Collins' cutter: phrase length 1..MaxPhraseLength bars; cut lengths odd units ≤ SubDiv/2 (1 or 3 8ths; 5 optional; 7 "would reveal too much of the original"); MaxRepeats 2; stutter probability 0.2; "SubDiv 8 works really well … syncopations dynamic enough to be interesting, but not so complex as to lose the listener".

**Povel & Essens counter-evidence.** The paper's clock selection uses C = W·s + u (s = clock ticks on silence, u = ticks on unaccented events, with W = 4 in the published version); W not re-verified from a fetched source.

## (d) Implications for a listener-model based on surprise and dwell time

**Computing rhythmic surprise from an onset list.** Use two layers, combined by precision weighting (PCRI):

1. *Schematic layer (meter prior).* Quantise onsets to a 16th grid per bar. Per onset, static surprise = LHL-type score: an onset at weight w_N followed by silence/tie at a stronger position w_R contributes (w_R − w_N); omissions on positions of weight 0 and −1 contribute the largest single terms (Bouwer's strong-beat MMN). Add the Sioros & Guedes amplitude term so ghost-note/velocity syncopation counts. Sum per bar → S_bar; normalise (NLHL-p) so bars of different density compare.
2. *Episodic layer (what this track has already taught).* Keep a table P[pos] of onset probability at each of the loop's grid positions (per instrument), updated each cycle with exponential decay (half-life ≈ 2–4 cycles, consistent with fast entrainment). Surprise of an onset or omission = −log₂ P or −log₂(1−P) (van der Weij). A syncopation that has appeared at the same position in the previous cycles has low episodic surprise even if its schematic score is high — this is Vuust's "expected deviations" and why a groove stays legible yet alive.
3. *Precision.* Estimate meter confidence from the best-clock counter-evidence (Povel & Essens) or the running S_bar: confidence ≈ 1 − S_bar/S_max. Effective surprise = schematic × episodic × confidence. When confidence collapses (S_bar in Witek's top tercile; onsets projecting a competing clock), surprise is discounted and the listener reports noise, or *resets* to the off-beat pulse (Fitch & Rosenfeld). A model should treat a reset as a change of pattern, not as a spike of surprise.

**How many bars until "understood".** Beat/phase within 1–2 cycles; a 1-bar pattern modelled by ~4 bars; a 2-bar break by ~8 bars. After that, episodic surprise → 0 and the listener is in the "understood" state; dwell time begins. The upper bound on comfortable dwell is set by hypermeter, not by memory decay: at 170 BPM a 4-bar unit is ~5.7 s (edge of the present) and 8 bars ~11.3 s. EDM practice (Butler, Smith, Solberg) places changes at 8/16/32-bar downbeats, i.e. every 11–45 s at 170 BPM. A practical rule: a rhythmic state that persists unchanged past 8 bars with no episodic surprise is in boredom territory; a state that changes every bar never lets the episodic table converge and is in the other failure mode (Senn/Witek high-syncopation illegibility).

**Legible variation vs noise.** A variation is legible when: it keeps the anchor (kick on the one; Hockman's downbeat-preserving edits); its events are ≥100 ms apart (8ths at 170 BPM yes, 16ths no — 16th chops read as roll/texture, legitimately a different kind of surprise, timbral not rhythmic); it displaces an existing event to a metrically related position rather than adding random onsets (LHL/Sioros "shift", Collins' odd-unit cuts that resolve at the downbeat); its bar-level S stays in the middle of the range (Matthews: index ~4 per bar of five onsets, not 18); and it lands on or just before a hypermetric boundary (a fill in bar 4/8 is expected *as a category* even when its content is new). Noise is: onsets that project no clock (high counter-evidence), sub-100 ms density without a backing beat, and per-bar changes that never recur, so the episodic layer never gets a chance to model them.

**Chopped vs continuous break.** A continuous Amen at 170 is a fixed 4-bar pattern (bars 1–2 identical, delayed snare in 3, empty downbeat + early crash in 4): it has moderate schematic syncopation but its episodic surprise reaches zero after two loops; thereafter all rhythmic surprise must come from other layers (bass, edits) or from the two-level legibility of the half-time bass (85 BPM tactus) against 8th-note hats. A grid-chopped break keeps the same timbral identity and the same downbeat anchor but re-orders slices in 1- and 3-unit cuts with repeats (Collins: MaxRepeats 2, stutter p = 0.2, phrases of 1–N bars), so each phrase injects fresh schematic and episodic surprise while confidence in the meter is held high by the grid and the kick. In model terms, chopping moves the break from the left of the inverted U (understood, dwell time growing) toward the middle, on a per-phrase schedule the producer controls; over-chopping (7-unit blocks reveal the original; 16th-level cuts without a backing beat; restarts on off-beats every bar) tips it into reset/noise. Two-step DnB is the opposite strategy: a low-syncopation, high-precision pattern at the half-time level that relies on hypermetric change and bass movement for its surprise. Both are consistent with the lead's theory; the numbers above give the grid on which to schedule the alternation.
