# Expectation, tension and reward in dance music: research brief

> Corpus strand 2 of 5. Secondary research, 2026-09-08, sources verified by
> fetch; evidence graded. Written against the project's listening model
> (pattern vs absence; two states; a boredom clock on each).

Framing used throughout: the listener oscillates between "I have understood this pattern" (settled) and "is a new pattern emerging?" (searching). Dwell time in either state beyond some limit produces disengagement. A pattern is *legible* when it can be discerned distinctly, regardless of loudness. "Surprise" is used for information-theoretic unexpectedness.

## (a) Core claims, with evidence quality

**1. The EDM break routine (breakdown → build-up → drop) is a deliberate expectation machine, and the drop is the peak-pleasure moment.**
Solberg (2014) analysed two tracks (Cinnamon Chasers "Body", Madeon "Icarus") and identified five techniques: uplifters (risers), the "drum roll effect" (progressive subdivision of the snare), large frequency-register shifts, removal-then-reintroduction of bass and kick, and a contrasting breakdown. She frames these explicitly through Meyer (1956) and Huron's ITPRA. Evidence: **weak** on its own (close reading of two tracks), but converted into testable hypotheses that were then confirmed:
- Solberg & Dibben (2019), N=24, skin conductance: significant main effect of section on integrated SCR (F=5.65, p<.01, η²=.197, large); drop > breakdown (p<.05) and drop > build-up (p<.01); breakdown vs build-up not different. 11 of 13 participants attributed pleasant bodily sensations in "Icarus" to build-up and drop; 83% reported a desire to move while seated. **Moderate** (small N, seated listeners, four tracks).
- Solberg & Jensenius (2017, EMR), 16 dancers, marker-based motion capture: pronounced changes in group quantity of motion (QoM) at breakdown, build-up and drop; build-up and drop rated most pleasurable and motivating to dance. Solberg & Jensenius (2019, *Musicae Scientiae*), 29 dancers in 3 groups, 10-minute DJ mix: a "sudden decrease and increase" in QoM across the break routine, and improved interpersonal synchronization (especially vertical movement) *after* the drop. **Moderate** (replicated across two cohorts; lab club).
- Turrell, Halpern & Javadi (2021), N=36 (33 analysed), EEG on 90 break-routine clips of 18–22 s: inferior and middle frontal gyri more active in the first 1 s of the drop, correlating with rated excitement; pre/post-central gyri and precuneus (motor/imagery) more active in the last 1 s of the build-up; a control shifted 4 s earlier showed no differences, so the effect is locked to the drop instant. **Moderate**.
- Grosz, Solberg et al. (2025) formalise the grammar: Intro/Breakdown–Buildup–Core–Outro maps onto Establisher–Initial–Peak–Release; the drop is a *punctual transition*, not a section; they distinguish a **tension peak** (end of build-up) from an **emotional-intensity peak** (at/after the drop, continuing into the core). **Weak–moderate** (theoretical, single worked example).

**2. Anticipation and peak are neurally separable; anticipation is itself rewarded.**
Salimpoor et al. (2011): PET showed striatal dopamine release at peak emotional arousal (chills); fMRI showed the caudate more active during anticipation and the nucleus accumbens at peak. **Strong** for the dissociation (landmark, widely replicated conceptually; N small). The primary text was not accessible to verify the exact anticipation window or percentage dopamine change; treat any specific number as unverified.

**3. Pleasure is a joint function of prospective uncertainty and retrospective surprise, not surprise alone.**
Cheung et al. (2019), 1,039 chords from 30 Billboard progressions (2.4 s each), n=39 behavioural + fMRI: the regression surface is a **saddle** — high pleasure when a chord is surprising in a low-uncertainty context, *or* unsurprising in a high-uncertainty context; low pleasure for the other two corners. Amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex track the interaction; NAcc encodes uncertainty. Gold et al. (2019, *J Neurosci*), N=43 + replication N=27: quadratic (inverted-U) effects of both information content and entropy on liking, outperforming linear; an interaction showing preference for more predictability in more uncertain contexts; seven repetitions lowered liking but did not remove the intermediate-complexity preference. Gold et al. (2019, *PNAS*): musical reward prediction errors engage NAcc and drive learning. Koelsch, Vuust & Friston (2019) supply the theory: music is an "epistemic offering" whose reward is uncertainty resolution. **Strong** (converging behavioural + imaging + formal models; caveat: harmonic/melodic stimuli, not rhythm or arrangement).

**4. Groove follows an inverted-U over rhythmic complexity (syncopation).**
Witek et al. (2014): 50 funk drum-breaks, modified Longuet-Higgins & Lee syncopation index 0–81; quadratic fits beat linear; medium syncopation maximised both wanting-to-move and pleasure; dancers rated everything higher; audio entropy added no explanatory power beyond syncopation. Stupacher/Matthews-style controlled set (PLoS ONE 2022): syncopation index 0 / 4 / 18 → groove 41.3 / 61.2 / 20.1 (F(2,1344)=686, η²=.46). Matthews et al. (2019): rhythm inverted-U replicated; harmonic complexity modulates it, high-complexity chords flatten the rhythm effect. Vuust & Witek (2014) give the mechanism: intermediate syncopation is "incongruent but not incompatible" with the metric model; too much and the model collapses, prediction error paradoxically falls. **Strong** for the inverted-U with synthetic stimuli; **contested** for real repertoire: Senn et al. (2018), 248 reconstructed drum patterns, found only linear, tiny effects of syncopation (R²=.010) and event density (R²=.011), no quadratic advantage, while style preference (R²=.123) and familiarity (η²=.051) dominated. Reading: the U exists when complexity is the only thing varied; in the wild it is swamped by taste.

**5. Microtiming is not what makes a groove.**
Senn et al. (2016): fully quantized and originally-performed microtiming were rated equally groovy; exaggerated deviations reduced groove; only experts were sensitive. Davies et al. (2013) similarly reported that added microtiming deviations did not help (abstract not retrievable this session). Danielsen's work (2006, 2010; "beat bins"; 2023 "Shaping rhythm") argues EDM producers work on a grid and shape groove through *sound* (envelope, timbre) rather than onset deviation, and that the pulse reference has temporal width rather than being a point. **Moderate** (converging null results; Danielsen is analytical/ethnographic).

**6. Preference and attention follow an inverted-U over complexity/familiarity (Berlyne), and this is robust.**
Chmiel & Schubert (2017): of 57 studies over 115 years, 87.7% compatible with a "segmented" inverted-U (you may be observing only the rising or falling limb), 8.8% mixed, 3.5% incompatible. Chmiel & Schubert (2018) show the model fails at collative extremes. Kidd et al. (2012, 2014): infants look away from sequences that are too predictable *or* too surprising ("Goldilocks"), replicated in audition. Gold 2019 above is the formal-model version. **Strong** as a phenomenon; **contested** as Berlyne's arousal mechanism.

**7. Interest is the rate of learning, not the amount of novelty.**
Schmidhuber (2009): interestingness = first derivative of compressibility; fully predictable and fully random data are both boring; noise is unpredictable but not interesting because it admits no compression progress. Pearce (2018): IDyOM information content explains up to 83% of variance in expectedness ratings; Agres, Abdallah & Pearce (2018): memory is better for predictable (low-IC) sequences, and by the third session IC explained 85% of memory variance, with unpredictable sequences becoming *worse* remembered with exposure. **Moderate** (Schmidhuber is theory; Pearce/Agres are strong for melody but not tested on arrangement-scale structure).

**8. Tension requires uncertainty plus stakes, and delaying resolution intensifies it.**
Lehne & Koelsch (2015): tension arises from conflict/instability/uncertainty about emotionally significant future events; intensity scales with the spread of possible outcome values; "delaying the resolution of the tension intensifies the tension experience" — flagged by the authors as under-studied. Huron (2006) ITPRA: tension response scales with uncertainty *and imminence*; surprise can be pleasant via contrastive valence (a fast negative reaction followed by a positive appraisal). Meyer (1956): affect arises when a tendency to respond is inhibited. **Strong** as theory; the delay-intensifies claim is **weak** empirically.

## (b) Measurable parameters and numbers

**Structural units.** Layers are introduced/changed/removed after 2, 4, 8, 16 or 32 bars (Solberg 2014; Solberg & Dibben 2019 stimulus criteria: 2/4/8/16 bars, 118–128 BPM four-to-the-floor). EDM tempo seldom changes; the "drum roll effect" produces the illusion of acceleration by subdividing note values (quarter → eighth → sixteenth) while the pulse stays fixed (Solberg 2014).

**Section lengths observed in analysed tracks.**
- "Body": Breakdown 2 = 16 s; Build-up 1 = 32 s; Build-up 2 = 16 s (≈8 / 16 / 8 bars at ~125 BPM). Drum roll in the last 4 bars of the build-up.
- "Icarus" (128 BPM): Breakdown 15–19 s, Build-up 15–17 s (≈8 bars each), drops at 69 s and 149 s, cores 45 s and 54 s, outro 8 s (Solberg 2014; Grosz et al. 2025). Drum roll culminates in five syncopated snare hits; bass slides down on beat 1; full groove returns on beat 2 after a "micro-vacuum".
- Solberg & Dibben's four house tracks: break routines 32 / 62 / 63 / 97 s (M=63.5 s, SD=26.6); build-ups 47 / 47 / 69 s (and ~15 s for "Icarus"). The shortest, loudest, most contrasted routine ("Icarus") produced the most detailed reports of bodily sensation.
- Turrell et al.: break routines "around 20 seconds"; clips 18–22 s with the drop at 12–16 s; drop passages 6 s.
- Yadati et al. (2014): 100 SoundCloud tracks; drop defined as the point where the build ends and the bassline enters; average automatic segment length 16.5 s; a second drop "highly unlikely" within 30 s of the first; only ~20% of user "drop" comments land within the tolerance window (crowd timestamps are noisy).
- Practitioner norms (not peer-reviewed; myloops.net trance blueprint at 138 BPM; KAN Samples dubstep at 140): build-up 16–32 bars, breakdown 64–96 bars (trance) or 16–32 bars (dubstep, longer only if dense), drop 32–64 bars split into 8- or 16-bar phrases with a change at each boundary. A worked 16-bar build: riser bars 1–8, second riser + gated pad bars 9–12, snare roll at eighths bars 13–14, sixteenths bar 15 with melodics removed, then **two beats of silence** before the drop.

**Drop contrast.** Drop tempo is unchanged; the contrast is amplitude, spectral flux and low-frequency register. Solberg & Dibben list four invariants: U-shaped amplitude profile (sudden dip at breakdown, gradual rise, maximum at drop); large frequency-register shift, especially at build-up→drop; removal then reintroduction of bass/kick; pitch-directed sweeps ascending into the drop and descending to mark it. Dubstep half-time (snare on beat 3 at 140 BPM) is the main tempo-*feel* contrast device in the genre literature; no peer-reviewed measurement found.

**Groove optimum.** Witek: peak at the middle tertile of a 0–81 syncopation index. Controlled set: index 4 (of 0/4/18) gave groove 61 vs 41 and 20 on a 0–100 scale. Senn: in real drum patterns, syncopation explains ~1% of variance; taste + familiarity ~15%.

**Inverted-U shape.** Gold 2019: quadratic in both IC and entropy; peak shifts toward *lower* IC as entropy rises. Cheung 2019: saddle, not a single U — the "sweet spot" is a diagonal (surprise × precision), not a point. Chmiel & Schubert: familiarity moves the peak rightward; complex pieces need more exposures to peak (Margulis 2014); some studies show liking still rising at 25 exposures, others peaking well before.

**Time-courses.** Caudate (anticipation) precedes NAcc (peak) by seconds (Salimpoor 2011; exact window unverified here). SCR is elevated in the drop window (−2 to +13 s around onset) relative to breakdown and build-up (Solberg & Dibben). Frontal EEG activity separates build-up from drop within 1 s but not 4 s earlier (Turrell). Group QoM drops abruptly at the breakdown and rises at the drop; synchrony improves *after* the drop (Solberg & Jensenius).

## (d) Implications for a listener-model based on surprise and dwell time

**What a drop is.** In this model the drop is not a surprise in the information-theoretic sense — every study above stresses the drop is *highly expected* (Solberg 2014: "the drop is inevitable"; Turrell: listeners know it will come but not exactly when or how). It is the resolution of a long, high-uncertainty *searching* state into a maximally legible pattern in one beat. Cheung's saddle explains why it is pleasurable despite low surprise: a low-surprise event in a high-uncertainty context is one of the two high-pleasure corners. The build-up manufactures the uncertainty (removed kick and bass remove the pulse anchor; risers and snare subdivision are non-repeating, so no pattern can be settled on); the drop supplies the confirmation. Grosz's two-peak distinction maps directly: the *tension peak* is the maximum of "is a new pattern emerging?" at the last bar of the build; the *emotional-intensity peak* is the moment of "I have understood this pattern" and it persists for the first phrase of the core. Salimpoor's caudate→NAcc handoff is the neural version of the same two states. Design consequence: the drop's payload must be legible within one bar — bass and kick back on the grid, the loudest and lowest content of the track, and, per Solberg & Dibben, a return to at least the pre-break amplitude. Madeon's trick of withholding the full groove for one beat after the bass slide (the "micro-vacuum", Grosz's "pre-drop break") is a final one-beat spike of uncertainty immediately before resolution; the two-beats-of-silence convention is the same device.

**What a breakdown is.** A breakdown is a deliberate *removal* of the pattern the listener has settled on — specifically the pulse-carrying layers — so that the listener is forced out of the settled state. It is not noise: it is low-density, low-entropy material (sustained chords, whole-note bass, filtered core) that is legible but *insufficient*, and its function is to reset the dwell clock. Contrast matters: Solberg & Dibben found the only track with a genuinely contrasting breakdown (new harmonic material rather than a thinned core) drew the most reported bodily sensation, and Grosz notes that the breakdown is less structurally obligatory than the build-up — what the grammar needs is the *cut* (abrupt loss of the core) followed by a build. In dwell-time terms: the breakdown ends the settled state abruptly (SCR does not rise here; QoM falls), then the build-up begins the search.

**How long a build can hold "is a new pattern emerging?".** The data cluster tightly: measured build-ups are 8–16 bars (15–47 s at 118–128 BPM; one 69 s outlier in a 97 s progressive-house routine), whole break routines average ~64 s, and Turrell's EEG-salient window is ~20 s. Practitioner norms say 16–32 bars for a build and cap breakdowns at 16–32 bars unless the arrangement is dense. The mechanism that sets the ceiling is Schmidhuber/Kidd: a searching state is only rewarding while the listener is making *compression progress*. A build sustains that by delivering a new, cheaply learnable regularity every 4–8 bars (riser enters, second riser, snare at eighths, snare at sixteenths, melodics stripped) — each is a small pattern that resolves quickly and predicts a bigger one. When no new regularity arrives, the search curdles: risers alone become stationary noise (no compression progress, boredom by Schmidhuber's definition), and an over-long snare roll at constant subdivision becomes a legible pattern of its own, which converts the search state back into a settled state without a payoff. Two numbers to carry: the last ~4 bars are where the subdivision must accelerate (Solberg's drum roll effect), and the 4-s-before-drop EEG control in Turrell showing nothing distinctive means the decisive uncertainty is concentrated in the last bar, not spread over the build. A reasonable working rule for a model: a build should present a new sub-pattern at least every 8 bars and must change the *rate* of change in the final 4; beyond ~32 bars without a resolution the literature offers no support and practitioner lore says it fails.

**Groove as steady-state dwell.** The core section is where the listener sits in "I have understood this" for 32–64 bars without disengaging. The syncopation inverted-U is the explanation: a medium-syncopated pattern keeps generating small, local prediction errors against a fully legible metre (Vuust & Witek's "incongruent but not incompatible"), so the listener is micro-cycling between the two states every bar while the macro-state stays settled. Stupacher's numbers give the target — the optimum is a *low* absolute syncopation (index ~4 out of a scale where 18 already collapses groove), i.e. a few off-beat events per bar against an unambiguous kick. Senn's null in real repertoire warns that once the pattern is familiar, taste and style recognition outweigh complexity; and Senn/Danielsen show microtiming is not the lever — grid-quantized patterns groove as well as played ones, and EDM producers shape feel with envelope and timbre. For a generator: vary sound, not onset offsets. Layer changes every 8 or 16 bars inside the core (the practitioner "change at every phrase boundary") are what stop the settled state from decaying into boredom — Gold's seven-repetition liking decline and Agres's finding that predictable material is *more* memorable (so it saturates faster) both point the same way.

**Does the inverted-U give usable thresholds?** Partly. It is robust as a shape (87.7% of 57 studies) but not as a fixed point: the peak shifts with familiarity (rightward with exposure, more exposures needed for more complex material) and with context (Gold: in high-entropy contexts listeners prefer *lower* surprise; Cheung: the optimum is a diagonal, not a point). The usable parts are (i) the syncopation numbers above for the steady state; (ii) the qualitative rule from Cheung/Gold that the amount of surprise a listener will tolerate is inversely proportional to how uncertain the context already is — so a build (high uncertainty) should end with something *expected* (the drop), whereas a settled core (low uncertainty) is where genuinely surprising events (a fill, a dropped kick, a new voice) pay off; (iii) the segmented-U warning that a model fitted to one population will see only one limb. No study gives a threshold in bars for when a settled pattern becomes boring; the closest proxies are the 8/16/32-bar phrase norms and Gold's seven-repeat decline, which together suggest expecting disengagement after roughly 4–8 unchanged repetitions of a 4-bar unit unless a new sub-pattern is introduced.
