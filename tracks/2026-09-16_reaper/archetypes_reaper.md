# Tim Reaper, the jungle revival, and the 90s archetype: what they say against what we measured

Research run 2026-09-16. We set what Tim Reaper and the 2010s-2020s jungle revival say about craft and
DJing against the seven measurements of `Tim_Reaper_Jungle_DJ_Set_SECTION_August_2026.wav`
([README.md](README.md), [alignment.md](alignment.md)). **No audio was fetched or used for this
file.** The only thing looked up about the set itself is its public title and channel.

**How to read the sourcing.** Numbers in brackets point to §4. **FETCHED** means the page was loaded,
but a small model summarised it, so paraphrases follow that summary, not a verbatim read.
**SNIPPET** means only a search-result summary was seen, which is weaker. There is one direct quote
from the artist; everything else is paraphrase. The web-search budget ran out partway through (the
session cap is 200 searches), so some corners of the brief went unsearched. They are listed in §4.

## 1. Who Tim Reaper is, and what he has said

**The person.** Tim Reaper is Ed Alloh, born 17 July 1993, from south-east London [12]. He found drum &
bass through an Andy C mix CD on the cover of Mixmag while doing a GCSE Media Studies assignment [9].
He came to oldskool jungle through a fake DJ Hype MySpace profile and learned it from forums, YouTube,
Discogs and Rolldabeats [11]. He had no musical training [1]. He works as a web developer at NTS [12],
which is sourced to his 2025 r/jungle AMA.

**Labels and collaborators.** In 2016 he and **Dwarde** started **Globex Corp**, a Simpsons-themed
white-label series run under Simon's **7th Storey Projects** [1][4][11]. It reached ten or more
volumes. Globex mixes a 92-93 hardcore style with 94-96 jungle, and names Basement, Creative Wax,
Formation and similar 90s labels as reference points [4]. **Future Retro London** began as a club night
he started because no London night was dedicated to the new jungle [8]. During the pandemic it became a
label, with the "Meeting Of The Minds" series pairing him with a different contemporary producer on
each release [1][8]. It won DJ Mag's Breakthrough Label in 2021 [12], put out about 50 releases, and was
paused indefinitely in October 2025. He said at the time that he wanted to focus on being himself [13].
His regular collaborators and peers include **Kid Lib** (Green Bay Wax, started 2013 and one of the
first labels for new oldskool jungle [42]), **Coco Bryce**, **Sully**, **Dev/Null** (with whom he has
co-run the biweekly BTTO radio show since about 2014 [4]), **Kloke** (the Hyperdub album *In Full
Effect*, September 2024 [17]), **FFF**, **Equinox** and **Basic Rhythm** [7][8][18-21]. The nights that
shaped him were **Rupture** (Corsica Studios), Jungle Syndicate, Technicality, and Kid Lib's Sheffield
nights [1]. Rupture's Double O calls him the definition of hard work paying off [23]. I found nothing
about **Sonic Shock**, **Nookie** or the Tim Reaper & Dwarde record **"Out Of Hand"** before the search
budget ran out.

**How he makes jungle.**
- **Tools.** He has used FL Studio since 2009 because it seemed the easiest way to chop breaks [1][2]. He
  works on a laptop with no outboard gear [1][3], and he produced on headphones, without speakers, until
  about 2020 [1]. One interview describes him making tunes on a train with earbuds [45].
- **Breaks come first.** The first skill he set out to learn was sequencing and chopping breakbeats.
  Loading samples and using VSTs came later, one step at a time [1][4]. Apart from the Amen, the break
  he names as a favourite is the Sesame Street break, for its snare and shuffle [9].
- **Samples.** He traces back to the original samples 90s producers used [8], but warns against
  sampling jungle to make jungle, since a narrow pool only regurgitates the sound [3]. His technique
  jumped once he started digging pre-1993 hardcore [3][11].
- **Workflow.** He usually starts from a sample, melody or hook, and prepares sounds before a session
  rather than experimenting inside it [1]. Since a low period in 2014 he has tended to finish a tune in
  the session he starts it [4]. With Dwarde he gets the bare bones down in a few hours and tweaks later
  [8]. Output is high: about 25 vinyl releases were planned for 2021 [1].
- **Aim: the 93-95 formula, plus new sounds.** He said: "The '93 to '95 period — that is the peak." [1]
  Because old-school jungle lasted only two or three years before turning into drum & bass, he feels
  that terrain was never fully explored [1]. He treats the oldskool style as a formula that new sounds
  can still be injected into [9], and as a balance between sounding like pastiche and drifting from the
  golden-era roots [5]. He wants the sound clean, but not so clean it seems dated [8]. In 2018 he said
  the new wave differs from the 90s in that producers mix every sub-style instead of committing to one,
  such as ragga or atmospheric [11]. He moves across jungle's timeline on purpose so listeners can't
  predict what comes next [3].
- **Bass.** He has said nothing technical about bass that I found. Writers put him among new-jungle
  producers built on big 808 bass (a DJ Mag 2018 line about the whole group, him included) [11]. They
  also describe heavy Reese basslines on his tracks [11], and in 2026 a bone-shaking Reese [16]. **Nothing on sidechain from him or any
  revival artist turned up.**

**How he DJs.**
- **Format.** He learned on other people's decks and bought a **Traktor controller**, which he still
  used in 2020. He keeps one turntable for checking test presses [4]. RA.757 was recorded on that
  controller in his living room [5]. On vinyl he plays mostly 90s favourites rather than his own or
  label material, and vinyl takes more effort and concentration [7]. In 2025 he said technology will
  let DJs move between genres without being tied to physical formats or a limited pitch range [6].
- **Selection.** He mixes old with new on purpose, because oldskool DJs tend to play only 90s records
  [4]. His mixes premiere forthcoming material from his own label and others [5]. DJ Mag's On Cue mix
  had only five tunes that were already released; the rest were forthcoming originals, unknowns and
  dubs from friends [3]. In RA.1000 he tried choosing tracks without his usual motive to play his own,
  his friends' or his label's [6]. He tries to balance challenging the crowd against entertaining it
  [4], and says the live back-and-forth with a crowd is unmatched [2].
- **Pace, from published tracklists.** RA.757: 34 tracks in 93:39, 9 of them his, none from the 90s
  [18]. On Cue: 22 in 59:31, 8 or more his [19]. Truancy 269: 22 in 63:10, 5 his and 3 from the 90s
  [20]. NTS, March 2021: about 22 in about an hour, 5 his and about 7 from the 90s [21]. That is
  **2.7-2.9 minutes per track, with 23-36% of tracks his own.**
- **Mixing technique.** I found no source on how he mixes: cuts or blends, doubles, key. Those rows
  in §2 therefore rest on the measurements.

### What the measurements found that no source addresses

- **Key.** E♭/D♯ minor holds 84.6% of bass time, with two whole-record excursions. I found no source on
  whether he mixes in key. The two E♭-minor tags on his remixes [40] are suggestive but only snippets.
  One mechanism is on record, though: playing 165 BPM tunes at 166 keeps their key, so a set built from
  one scene's 165-BPM E♭-minor records stays in key without any deliberate key mixing.
- **Zero risers.** No revival source says "no risers." The 90s-style subtraction build shows up only in
  a writer's image (row 10) and in Sully's preference for notes over automation (row 11). The
  counter-archetype, riser plus snare roll, comes from modern DnB templates (the research brief, §5).
- **Layer clocks** (riff ~8, sub tone ~17, kick ~28, carrier ~32 bars). No source describes
  arrangement at this level.

### Which records might be his

This can't be known from what I could reach. The YouTube page for the SECTION set exposed only its title
and channel (`@section_hq`) [39]. A search snippet dates the upload to 3 August 2026 [43]. Its
description or tracklist wasn't retrievable, and MixesDB has no entry for it [MixesDB category]. His
published mixes from 2020-21 run **23-36% his own productions, remixes and collaborations** [18-21], and
Crack Mix 415 has at least 8 [22]. By that ratio, **2 or 3 of the 9 records** would be his. The
structural family of T1, T3, T5 and T7 (the bassy records) is a resemblance, not an attribution. The
airlock at bar 378 fits his DJ-tool releases (row 9), but that isn't evidence either.

## 3. What the revival keeps from the 90s archetype, and what it changes

**The archetype, briefly.** Jungle in 1993-95 ran at about 155-165 BPM [31]. The rhythm was chopped
breakbeats, above all the Amen, edited on Akai S950s and Ataris or Amigas [29][30]. Bass was 808 or sine
subs, Reese, or oddities such as Dred Bass [30], with dub and reggae roots [32]. Tunes were cut to
dubplates to play that weekend [33], with rewinds called by MCs [36]. After 1996 drum & bass moved to
170-180 BPM [31] and a darker, cleaner sound [32]. The two-step roller, repetitive drums changing every
16 or 32 bars [37], and rapid three-deck double-drop mixing [35] belong to that later drum & bass
archetype.

### Kept

| trait | evidence from the revival | seen in the set |
|---|---|---|
| **The 93-95 tempo band** | His peak era is 93-95 [1]; Dead Man's Chest slows back down to the older tempo [11]; his remixes at 165 [40, SNIPPET] | 166 BPM (row 1) |
| **Chopped breaks as the rhythm, not a programmed two-step** | He learned break chopping first [1][4]; a favourite classic break [9]; the writer's rejection of modern DnB rhythm structures [11]; Coco Bryce left over 2-step [25] | break kick 58%, 16ths carried by the break, A-B-C-D bars (rows 17-18) |
| **Sub weight and space, not drum impact** | Drums set back, room for subs [11]; booming 808 bass [11]; 808s or sines through samplers [30] | drop = sub +10 dB with falling centroid; clean sub; 808-like ping (rows 21-22) |
| **Separation by register, not ducking** | No explicit source. It follows from sampler-era practice: Kid Lib's S950 and desk [42, SNIPPET], Woolford's Akai and E-mu [27] | no sidechain (row 25) |
| **Drops by subtraction** | Breakdowns heard as plunges, not drops [3]; notes over automation [24] | 82% subtraction, zero risers (rows 10-11) |
| **Original samples** | He traces the 90s producers' own sources [8]; Woolford relearned how the old tracks were made [27] | not measurable |
| **Dubplate culture** | Mixes built from dubs and forthcoming tunes [3][5]; vinyl releases and test presses [1][4] | not measurable (row 8) |
| **The raw sound** | Rawness over sterility [11]; grit without studious recreation [17] | transients survive at 0 dBFS (row 26) |

### Changed

| change | evidence | seen in the set |
|---|---|---|
| **One producer, every sub-style** | Producers now blend ragga, atmospheric, hardcore and more instead of committing to one [11]; Globex mixes 92-93 and 94-96 [4]; he moves across eras so listeners can't predict [3]; he names hardcore and techno as ingredients [8] | bass attack chosen per record; kick class changes 6 times; a two-step record used as a contrast (rows 16-17) |
| **Picking up where 93-95 left off, not following it into DnB** | Unexplored terrain because the style lasted only 2-3 years [1]; a formula open to new sounds [9]; pastiche vs. roots [5] | the grammar measured is 93-95 throughout; the "new" part (sound design, samples) doesn't show up in structural numbers |
| **Laptop and DAW, not the hardware studio** | FL Studio on a laptop, headphones [1][3]; Sully works in a Renoise tracker [16] | a quantised grid with human-scale microtiming, nothing looped (row 18) |
| **Speed and volume** | Finishes tunes in one session [4]; bare bones in hours [8]; about 25 vinyl releases in a year [1]; about 50 Future Retro releases in 5 years [13] | short, dense records of 46-192 bars |
| **Digital DJing on one clock** | Traktor controller [4][5]; vinyl kept for 90s sets [7]; no pitch-range limits [6] | 166 BPM ±0.03 for 21 min (row 3). A 90s vinyl set would drift. |
| **A new-music-first set** | Mixing old and new is his stated aim [4]; 23-36% his own; RA.757 had no 90s records [18] | not measurable, but a digital set by his own account leans new |
| **DJ craft borrowed from DnB rather than jungle** | Rapid mixing and double drops are the Andy C drum & bass lineage [35] | cuts prepared by bass removal; doubled drums before a drop; a 32-bar double-play (row 5) |
| **Rewinds** | A jungle and MC tradition [36] | none in 21 min (row 7) |

**Net reading.** Most of what the measurement found is the 93-95 archetype as the revival describes
it: tempo, the break as the rhythm, sub weight over drum impact, subtraction drops, and no ducking.
The revival changes three things: it mixes sub-styles inside one set or one tune, it produces and
releases fast on a laptop, and it DJs on a digital clock with quick drum & bass-style mixing. The
one clear drum & bass element in the rhythm itself, a two-step record with a still bass, is used as
a contrast, not as the norm.
